The area is 8 times larger, but the cost is 20 times higher. A big chip loses twice: once on yield and once at the wafer edge. Lay dies out on a wafer and count them yourself, then change the defect density and how strongly defects cluster to see how the cost per die moves.

Splitting into just ten cells already gets you to 97% of the answer. The stronger the clustering (the smaller α), the higher the yield, even though the expected total number of defects is the same. With α = 1 the standard deviation of the defect density equals the mean; with α = 5 it is about 45% of the mean. Yield is a question of "how many dies are contaminated", not "how many defects there are".

What this calculation leaves out

  • Edge exclusion and scribe line width are set to 0. Real wafers have both, so a direct count would also come out lower than this.
  • Good dies and cost are computed as in the solution to Exercise 2, by multiplying the GDPW approximation (√(2A)) by the yield. For 100–800 mm² dies this formula gives 1–5% fewer dies than a direct count.
  • Strictly, the A in λ = AD₀ is the critical area, not the die area. Using the die area as is folds the effect of the critical area into the density, a simplification.
  • e−AD₀ counts defects only. Parametric yield, the subject of speed and power binning, is not explained by this formula.
  • Cost per die is the silicon cost only. HBM, packaging and test come on top of it. The wafer price of USD 15,000 is an assumed value that the text introduces with "suppose".
