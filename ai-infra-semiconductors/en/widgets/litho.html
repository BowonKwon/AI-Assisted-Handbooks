Four strands of lithography numbers, moved on one board: the linewidth and depth of focus set by the Rayleigh criterion, the photons that run short as the price of a shorter wavelength, the yield that multiplies in series over hundreds of steps, and the size of the field that reduction projection allows in a single exposure.

EUV gains 14.3× from wavelength and loses 4.1× from NA, for a net gain of 3.5×. Because its NA is lower, its depth of focus is actually 17% more generous than that of ArF immersion. The real problem is High-NA. DOF shrinks to 0.36× that of EUV and 0.42× that of ArF immersion, about 22 nm. A 50 nm photoresist is already thicker than the DOF.

Throughput limit

"Limit" is the throughput when exposure time dominates the total. With a fixed time T₀ for wafer transfer and alignment, the drop is smaller, and how much smaller depends on how large T₀ is relative to the original exposure time (if T₀ equals the exposure time, throughput falls to 13%; if it is ten times as long, to 45%). So in practice the dose is not raised 14.3-fold to match the photon count. ASML rates EUV tool throughput at 30 mJ/cm² (NXE:3600D, 3800E) and 50 mJ/cm² (High-NA EXE:5000), and better resist sensitivity, design workarounds and metrology make up the rest.

③ Multiplying 800 steps

Per-step yield y = Y1/n　·　allowed defect rate = 1 − y　·　inverse: line yield = yn ≈ e−(1−y)n

Allowed defect rate per step

When the step count grows 1.5×, the allowed defect rate falls to 2/3. A per-step yield of 99% sounds excellent, yet after 800 steps almost nothing comes out. Hundreds of steps multiply in series, so each step must be perfect to the ppm, not "nearly perfect". A decision that adds steps (multi-patterning) adds yield risk on top of cost.

The field is halved so that the 6-inch mask can stay. The 104 × 132 mm area on the mask is unchanged; only the direction reduced 8× becomes 132 ÷ 8 = 16.5 mm. High-NA draws finer patterns but halves the area drawn in one exposure. An 800 mm²-class die cannot be printed in one High-NA field, so it needs stitching or chiplets.

What this calculation leaves out

  • k₁ = 0.3 is near the practical floor for single exposure (theoretical floor about 0.25). Finer patterns are split over several exposures with multi-patterning. That goes around k₁ rather than lowering it.
  • k₂ is held the same for all three generations. This is the assumption of Exercise 2.
  • Throughput is not plotted. The text gives throughput = 1/(T₀ + texp), but no value for the fixed time T₀. So only the exposure-time ratio and the required source power move, and throughput appears only as its limit when exposure time dominates everything (1/m), marked "limit".
  • Photon arrival is treated as a Poisson process (relative fluctuation 1/√N). Resist improvements that let one photon trigger more reactions (chemical amplification, metal-oxide resists) are left out. Dot counts in the photon figure show ratios only.
  • Line yield assumes total yield is the product of step yields. The inverse curve and readouts use the exact formula yn, as the table in the text does. The small-defect-rate approximation e−(1−y)n is noted in small type in the readout.
  • Largest-die yield uses the Poisson yield formula of Session 4. Stochastic defects add directly to this D₀. A mask defect repeats at the same spot in every field printed with that mask, so it behaves differently from random defects.
